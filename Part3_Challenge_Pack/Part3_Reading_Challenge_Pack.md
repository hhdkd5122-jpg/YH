## Part 3A • Practice 1 | Money Talks | Consumerism and the human brain

### The Decoy in the Middle

*B2.1 • 312 words • 3 marks per question*

**[1]** Order a coffee in a Dubai café and you will usually be offered three sizes. Most customers choose the medium, and many believe they have made an independent, sensible decision. In reality, the small and large cups are often designed less to be sold than to make the medium look reasonable. Marketers call the least attractive option on such a menu the decoy.

**[2]** The best-known demonstration came from a magazine subscription offer. Readers could buy online access for 59 dollars, the print edition for 125 dollars, or print and online together, also for 125 dollars. Almost nobody chose print alone, yet when researchers removed that option, the share choosing the combined package fell from 84 per cent to 32 per cent. An option that hardly anyone wanted had changed what most people bought.

**[3]** The decoy works because the brain finds absolute value difficult to judge but relative value easy. Faced with two options that differ in several ways, we hesitate. Add a third that is clearly worse than one of them, and that one suddenly appears to be the obvious winner. The comparison feels like reasoning, although the menu has done most of the work.

**[4]** Decoys are not always dishonest. A restaurant may genuinely sell its largest portion, and a phone company may offer three plans because customers need different amounts of data. The difficulty is that a shopper cannot easily tell whether the middle option exists to serve customers or to steer them, and the effect works in either case.

**[5]** The practical response is to remove the decoy mentally. Before choosing, cover the option you would never buy and ask whether you would still prefer the same item from the two that remain. If the answer changes, the menu, not your preference, was making the decision. Shoppers who perform this small test rarely pay for a size they did not want.

### Subscriptions You Forgot

*B2.1 • 305 words • 3 marks per question*

**[1]** Check the bank statement of almost any young professional in Abu Dhabi and you are likely to find small monthly payments that the owner barely remembers: a streaming service, a fitness app, a cloud storage plan. Each costs only a few dirhams, and that is precisely the problem. Individually they are too small to notice; together they can exceed the cost of a weekly family meal.

**[2]** Subscription businesses depend on a predictable feature of human behaviour, which economists call status quo bias. People tend to keep whatever arrangement is already in place, not because they have chosen it again, but because changing it requires a decision. Cancelling demands effort today, while the cost of continuing is spread thinly across the future, where the brain gives it little weight.

**[3]** Free trials exploit this tendency especially well. A customer signs up intending to cancel before the first payment, enters card details and then forgets. Research on trial offers suggests that a large share of people who plan to cancel never do. Companies know this, which is why many trials end automatically in a paid plan instead of simply expiring.

**[4]** Regulators in several countries have responded by requiring that cancelling be as easy as joining, and some now oblige companies to send a reminder before a free trial becomes a paid one. These rules help, but they do not remove the underlying bias. A reminder that arrives among dozens of other emails can be ignored as easily as the payment itself.

**[5]** The most effective defence is to reverse the default. Instead of asking whether to cancel, ask whether you would sign up today, at this price. Several financial advisers recommend reviewing every subscription twice a year with exactly this question. Services that pass the test are worth keeping. Those that fail were never really chosen; they simply continued.

## Part 3A • Practice 3 | Money Talks | Consumerism and the human brain

### Money with Labels

*B2.1 • 306 words • 3 marks per question*

**[1]** Imagine two people who each receive AED 500. The first gets it as an unexpected bonus at work; the second has saved it, dirham by dirham, over several months. Economically, the two sums are identical, since money carries no label. Psychologically, however, they are treated very differently. The bonus is far more likely to be spent quickly, and on something the person would not normally buy.

**[2]** The economist Richard Thaler described this tendency as mental accounting. People divide their money into invisible categories, such as rent, savings, holidays or gifts, and apply different rules to each. Money in one category is spent freely, while an identical amount in another is protected carefully, even when moving it would make obvious financial sense.

**[3]** The categories can be useful. A family that keeps a separate account for school fees is less likely to spend that money on something else, and many people find that a clear limit for entertainment helps them control their spending. In this sense, mental accounting is a simple form of self-discipline that works with human nature instead of against it.

**[4]** The same habit can also be expensive. Some people keep savings earning almost no interest while paying high interest on a credit card, because the savings feel untouchable and the debt belongs to a different category. A gift of money at Eid may be spent on luxuries even by a household struggling with bills, because gift money seems to belong to pleasure, not necessities.

**[5]** The solution is not to abandon mental categories but to review them. Ask whether a particular label is protecting you or costing you. Money that arrives unexpectedly can be given a category before it arrives, such as debt repayment or emergency savings. The labels we attach to money are invented by us, which means we are also free to change them.

### The Sound of Spending

*B2.1 • 303 words • 3 marks per question*

**[1]** Walk into a large shopping centre in Dubai on a busy evening and almost everything you experience has been planned: the temperature, the lighting, the music and, increasingly, the smell. Retailers describe this as atmosphere. Psychologists prefer a more precise term, sensory marketing, because its purpose is not simply to make shoppers comfortable but to influence how long they stay and how much they spend.

**[2]** Music offers the clearest evidence. In a well-known supermarket study, slower background music led shoppers to walk more slowly and to spend noticeably more than faster music did. Most of the shoppers, when asked afterwards, could not remember whether any music had been playing at all. The effect worked precisely because it went unnoticed.

**[3]** Scent operates in a similar way. Several hotel chains and clothing stores now use a signature fragrance, released through the air-conditioning, so that customers associate the smell with the brand. Because smell is closely linked to memory and emotion, a pleasant scent can make a shop seem more welcoming and its products seem more valuable, even when nothing about the products has changed.

**[4]** Not every technique succeeds. A scent that is too strong can irritate customers, and music that does not suit the shop, such as loud dance tracks in a jewellery store, can make shoppers leave sooner. Sensory marketing works best when it fits what customers already expect, which is why luxury boutiques tend to be quiet, cool and softly lit.

**[5]** None of this is illegal, and much of it makes shopping more pleasant. The point is to recognise that a decision made in a carefully designed space is partly made by the space. A useful habit is to leave the shop before deciding on any large purchase. If the item still seems worth the price outside, the decision is probably your own.

### Buying Time

*B2.1 • 305 words • 3 marks per question*

**[1]** Most discussions about money and happiness focus on how much people earn. A growing body of research suggests that a more useful question is what people do with the money they already have. One finding in particular has attracted attention: people who spend money to save time tend to report greater satisfaction with their lives than those who spend it on material goods.

**[2]** In one experiment, working adults were given a small sum of money on two separate weekends. On one weekend they were told to buy something material for themselves; on the other, to buy something that would save them time, such as a meal delivery or help with cleaning. On the time-saving weekend, participants reported a better mood and less stress by the end of the day.

**[3]** The effect appeared across income levels, not only among the wealthy. Yet surprisingly few people take advantage of it. In surveys, even many participants with comfortable incomes said they rarely paid to avoid unpleasant tasks. Some felt guilty about paying others for work they could do themselves; others simply did not think of time as something money could buy.

**[4]** The researchers are careful about their conclusions. An hour gained from a delivery service and then spent scrolling through a phone offers little advantage.

**[5]** For people whose basic needs are met, however, the message is practical. Before the next purchase, ask whether the money would be better used to remove a task you dislike. In a busy city such as Dubai, where commuting and long working hours are common, an hour returned may be worth more than another object on a shelf.

*B2.2 • 320 words • 2 marks per question*

**[1]** Walk through the toy section of a large store and the division is often visible from a distance. One side is dominated by pink and purple, with dolls, kitchens and craft sets; the other by blue, black and green, with construction kits, vehicles and science experiments. Young children cannot yet read the signs, but they rarely need to. The colours tell them which aisle is intended for them.

**[2]** Researchers have long debated whether such preferences are learned or inborn. Studies of very young infants find only modest differences between boys and girls in what they look at or reach for. By the age of three or four, however, the differences have become considerably larger, and children frequently justify their choices by declaring that a toy is 'for boys' or 'for girls'.

**[3]** This matters for careers because toys are not merely entertainment; they are early practice. Construction sets develop spatial reasoning, the capacity to picture how objects fit together and move, which is strongly associated with success in engineering and architecture. Dolls and role-play develop language and social skills valuable in teaching, medicine and management. A child who plays with one kind of toy practises one set of skills.

**[4]** Some retailers have responded by reorganising shelves by theme or age instead of gender, and a few governments have encouraged them to do so. Critics argue that such changes are cosmetic, since children still absorb expectations from family, television and friends. Supporters reply that the shelf is one of the few places where a message can be altered quickly and cheaply.

**[5]** The evidence does not suggest that boys and girls are identical, nor that every difference is imposed by marketing. It suggests something more modest: that small early tendencies can be enlarged by the signals surrounding them. If future engineers and nurses are partly shaped in the toy aisle, the way that aisle is arranged is considerably less trivial than it appears.

### Women at Sea

*B2.2 • 314 words • 2 marks per question*

**[1]** Of the roughly 1.9 million seafarers who crew the world's merchant ships, women make up little more than one per cent, according to industry estimates. Most of them work on cruise ships, in catering and hotel roles, rather than on the bridge or in the engine room. On cargo vessels, which carry most of the world's trade, a female officer may spend months as the sole woman aboard.

**[2]** The reasons are partly practical. Voyages can last many months, ships are isolated, and accommodation was originally designed for all-male crews. Yet the more persistent barrier is cultural. Shipping has long been described as a man's world, and that description has shaped who applies to maritime academies, who is hired after graduation and who is eventually promoted to command.

**[3]** The industry now has an increasingly urgent reason to change. Many countries report shortages of qualified officers, and an industry that recruits from half the population is, in effect, competing for talent with one hand tied behind its back. International maritime organisations have therefore launched programmes to attract and retain women, including mentoring schemes and annual awards.

**[4]** Retention is as important as recruitment. Women who join the industry frequently leave earlier than men, citing isolation, harassment and the difficulty of combining long voyages with family life. Several shipping companies have responded with shorter contracts, stricter conduct policies and rules ensuring that no woman is the sole female member of a crew, although such measures remain far from universal.

**[5]** In the Gulf, where trade and ports are central to national economies, the question carries particular weight. Emirati women have begun to graduate as marine engineers and deck officers, and their visibility matters. A teenager who has never seen a woman in a ship's uniform is unlikely to imagine herself in one. The sea has not changed; what is changing is who feels able to picture themselves on it.

### Hard Hats

*B2.2 • 311 words • 2 marks per question*

**[1]** The construction sites of the UAE are among the most visible workplaces in the world, and for decades almost everyone working on them was a man. That is beginning to change, though unevenly. Women now form a substantial share of engineering and architecture graduates in the country, yet they remain considerably more common in design offices than on the sites where their designs are built.

**[2]** Employers often explain the gap by referring to conditions. Sites are hot, noisy and physically demanding, and work frequently begins before sunrise. These conditions are real, but they apply equally to men, and women who choose site roles describe them as a challenge rather than a barrier. The more significant obstacle, many report, is the expectation that they will not stay.

**[3]** That expectation can become self-fulfilling. A manager who assumes that a young female engineer will soon move to an office may give her fewer site responsibilities, which in turn leaves her less qualified for promotion on site and more likely, eventually, to move to an office. The original assumption then appears to have been correct, when in fact it helped to produce the result it predicted.

**[4]** Several large developers in the region have begun to break this cycle by setting targets for women in site management, providing proper facilities and pairing new engineers with experienced mentors. Results have been encouraging but uneven. Where senior managers actively support the policies, women stay and are promoted; where the policies exist mainly on paper, little changes.

**[5]** Construction is sometimes presented as the clearest example of a man's job, because it is so closely associated with physical strength. Yet the decisive skills on a modern site are planning, coordination and technical judgement. The hard hat was never really measuring muscle. For a long time it was measuring assumptions, and those, unlike concrete, can be reshaped within a single generation.
